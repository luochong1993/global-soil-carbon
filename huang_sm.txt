Materials and Methods
Soil carbonate system. SIC exists in soils in various forms, including gaseous carbon dioxide,
dissolved inorganic carbons (CO2, H2CO3, HCO-3 and CO2-3) and solid carbonates (primarily calcium
carbonate, CaCO3). Solid carbonates are the predominant component by weight, and we refer to SIC
as solid carbonates when quantifying soil carbon stocks. Solid carbonates can be further categorized
into geogenic or lithogenic carbonates inherited from calcareous soil parent material (such as
limestone) and pedogenic carbonates formed through dissolution-precipitation of existing carbonates
or precipitation of Ca2+ from silicate weathering. The dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC) contributes
only a small portion of total SIC, but is closely coupled with the partial CO2 pressure (pCO2) of the
soil atmosphere to determine the persistence (dissolution vs. precipitation) of solid carbonate through
the following two chemical equations:
                 H2O + CO2 (g) ↔ H2CO3 (aq) ↔ HCO3‾ (aq) + H+                       (S1)
                CaCO3 (s)+ H+ ↔ HCO3‾ (aq) + Ca2+ (aq)                              (S2)
Measurement data collection and standardization. Following the convention, we quantify SIC as
12% of the calcium carbonate equivalent in fine earth (i.e., particle size ≤ 2 mm) (51), in which non-
calcium (e.g., magnesium) carbonate is included. Site-level measurements (roughly from 1980 to
2020) were collected from the ISRIC World Soil Information Service (WoSIS) with standardised soil
profile data, covering sample locations, soil depths, soil inorganic carbon, soil organic carbon, soil
texture, bulk density, soil pH, volumetric coarse fractions, cation exchange capacity and other soil
properties(51). WoSIS profile data were supplemented by additional databases from National Soil
Surveys, resamplings and new measurements from China(4, 52), from literature via “China National
Knowledge Infrastructure” (https://www.cnki.net/) and “Web of Science”
(http://www.isiknowledge.com/), from additional samples collected in South-West Germany (53),
Egypt(54) and Russia. Details of the database and processes of data standardization are given in
Supplementary Text.
Acquisition and preprocessing of spatial data. To develop predictive models, we selected
geospatial covariates based on their relevance to processes that affect SIC dynamics through
reviewing existing studies(17, 41, 55). We targeted on integrating prior knowledge gained from
multiple mechanistic and experimental studies on predictors of SIC storages. We combine
observations with existing (“prior”) knowledge to “constrain” the predictor-response relationships,
which form the basis to predict SIC under different conditions. We therefore did not apply methods
such as recursive feature elimination to optimize feature selections based purely on the overall
numerical information from data, to avoid excluding potential predictors especially when
interpretating patterns at the regional scale. We selected factors reflecting soil properties (soil pH,
nutrient contents/additions, soil erosion, cation exchange capacity, soil texture, coarse fragments, bulk
density, electrical conductivity, soil organic carbon and water table depth) together with five major
factors contributing to soil formation processes and therefore SIC storage, including parent material,
topography, living organisms, climate and hydrology (Table S2). These covariates were derived
through ground-based stations, satellite-based remote sensing, atmospheric and hydrological
reanalysis, soil and lithological maps and the digital elevation model. Considering the clear pattern of
observed SIC with soil depth and its role in driving soils’ diffusion capacity, we also included the
upper and lower soil depths as covariates.
         Topographic covariates including elevation, topographic wetness index (TWI), plan
curvature, profile curvature, relative slope position, convergence index and slope, were generated
from Multi-Error-Removed Improved-Terrain DEM. TWI that quantifies topographic controls on
hydrological processes was obtained from ref (56), while others were derived with SAGA GIS (Table
S2). Landform classes were obtained from the USGS global ecosystem map. Biological covariates
included gross primary productivity, net primary productivity, soil respiration, maximum rooting
depth and monthly maximum leaf area index. We grouped ESA-CCI land cover(57) into bare land,
agricultural lands, grassland and tree-dominated (tree and shrub) pixels. Climatic and hydrological
covariates included annual mean temperature, annual precipitation, different variations of temperature
and precipitation, the aridity index, reference (potential) evapotranspiration, wind speed, soil
temperature, soil water, runoff, water table depth, actual evapotranspiration, climate water deficit,
downward surface shortwave radiation, vapor pressure and the Palmer drought severity index. We
used the USGS global ecosystem map to represent parent materials. Detailed information about these
covariates is provided in Table S2.
         We preprocessed all spatial covariates and resampled to match the target resolution used
during analysis (nominal 1-km2 pixels), and spatially cohered using the nearest neighbour resampling
where necessary. For soil covariates that are associated with depth, we standardized values of
corresponding variables at depths of 0-30cm, 30-100cm and 100-200cm by calculating the weighted
averages if there are multiple depth intervals (e.g., 0-5cm, 5-10cm, 10-30cm for 0-30cm) within each
of our three standard depths according to the trapezoidal rule(58) when necessary.
Training, validation and statistical analysis. From the observation database, a large proportion of
the measured SIC values were zero (Fig. 1), and this could lead to fitted models with predictions
biased toward the zero. We therefore took two steps similarly as the two-part models in statistics(59).
First, we decomposed each training dataset into two classes: no SIC (SIC = 0, N=94,945) and with the
presence of SIC (SIC > 0, N=128,648). Then, a binary classification algorithm was trained to estimate
the occurrence probability of each class to determine whether the target had SIC different from zero
or not. In the next step, the regression model was built upon data with the presence of SIC to predict
the magnitude of SIC content.
         Tenfold cross-validation was used to estimate the performance of fitted models. Area under
the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was used to measure the ability of a classifier to
distinguish between classes. The higher AUC indicates the better performance of the classification
model. In addition to AUC, misclassification accuracy, precision, recall and F1-score (the harmonic
mean of precision and recall) metrics were also calculated for the fitted classifier models (Table S3).
For regression predictions, root-mean-squared error (RMSE) and R-squared (R2) were estimated.
         The trained classifier with the highest AUC and F1-score and the regression model with the
smallest RMSE and highest R-squared were selected for the rest of the analysis. With a combination
of hyperparameter tuning, gradient boosting outperformed extreme gradient boosting and random
forest methods for the classification and regression tasks (Table S3). The selected models showed
similar performance for predicting SIC in both surface and subsurface layers (Fig. S20).
Global prediction and SIC budgets. The classification and regression models selected above were
applied to predict SIC content for 3 soil layers (0-0.3m, 0.3-1m and 1-2m) individually at the nominal
1-km2 resolution. The top 2m SIC stock of each pixel was estimated as,

where 𝐵𝐷𝑖 is the bulk density in each soil layer, 𝐷𝑖 is the actual thickness of the estimated soil layer,
and 𝐶𝐹𝑉𝑂𝑖 is the volumetric fraction of coarse fragments. For pixels with shallow soils, 𝐷𝑖 is inferred
from the depth to bedrock. To estimate country-level budgets, country borders are based on TM
World Borders Dataset 0.3. For the budgets, we estimated the uncertainties combing modelling
uncertainty through repeated training of the models in 10-fold cross-validation and uncertainties in
𝐵𝐷, 𝐷 and 𝐶𝐹𝑉𝑂 (58).
Future scenarios and soil pH. Multiple sources contribute to changing soil pH status. Here we
focused on nitrogen additions and physical climate changes as they are the top two most important
drivers considering the magnitude and extent of their influence on soil pH globally(25, 60). We did
not quantify, for example, the impact of cation uptake by plants, the shift of biological nitrogen
fixation, sulfur deposition and liming on future soil pH changes due to large uncertainties in their
roles in regulating soil pH under heterogeneous soil environment, limited availability of global
datasets for their future projections, or their relatively smaller impact at the global scale. The impact
of rising atmospheric CO2 levels on oceanic pH and carbon dynamics is well-documented(61).
However, the effects of elevated atmospheric CO2 on soil pH are more complex and depend on
multiple factors, including soil-air connectivity and biological CO2 generation or consumption. As a
result, predicting the effects of higher atmospheric CO2 on soil pH accurately is challenging, and this
study does not assess these effects.
         We provide a range of soil pH estimates driven by future climate changes under three Shared
Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs), with greenhouse gas emission scenarios from low to high: SSP1-2.6
(low-emission), SSP2-4.5 (intermediate-emission) and SSP5-8.5 (high-emission). We emphasize
estimates from SSP5-8.5, because the recent, historical global warming trajectory aligns closely with
SSP5-8.5(62) and to demonstrate the largest impact from unmitigated emissions.
         Climate-driven pH changes by the end of this century (Fig. S15) were obtained from
predictions of the XGBoost regression model (R-squared of 0.85; 10-fold cross-validation), which
was built upon 181,955 soil pH measurements to link the spatial variations of soil pH to climatic
(mean annual precipitation, reference evapotranspiration and mean annual temperature), edaphic,
topographic and other environmental variables. The XGBoost regression model predicted soil pH
under future climate assuming edaphic and topographic factors remain relatively unchanged. Mean
annual precipitation and reference evapotranspiration ranked as the top two most important predictors.
The primary role of hydrology in predicting soil pH, revealed by the XGBoost regression, is well-
supported by ref (25) that attributes the global pattern of soil pH to water-balance. Changes in soil pH
were quantified as the difference of soil pH by the end of this century (average climate conditions,
2091-2100) compared to the present-day (2015-2024).
         Future (by 2100) climate were predicted by models participating in the Coupled Model
Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6). Given the availability of the data for the required
variables, projections from sixteen global models (ACCESS-CM2, AWI-CM-1-1-MR, CAS-ESM2-0,
CESM2-WACCM, CMCC-CM2-SR5, CNRM-CM6-1-HR, EC-Earth3-Veg-LR, FGOALS-g3, FIO-
ESM-2-0, GFDL-ESM4, INM-CM5-0, IPSL-CM6A-LR, MIROC6, MPI-ESM1-2-LR, MRI-ESM2-0,
NorESM2-MM) were employed to estimate soil pH.
         Future (by 2050) changes in soil pH driven by nitrogen additions were quantified through the
empirical relationship between cumulative N fertilizer inputs over 30 years (Nin, MgN ha-1) and the
corresponding pH reductions (ΔpH) derived from national soil surveys in two time periods in China
(Figure 7 of ref (63), ∆𝑝𝐻 = −0.06𝑁𝑖𝑛 + 0.11 ). This relationship captures responses of soil pH to
nitrogen additions under field conditions. A majority of Chinese croplands have experienced
significant soil acidification primarily due to fertilization and to a lesser extent by nitrogen deposition
over the past several decades(64). The relationship used in the study covers a wide range of fertilizer
inputs and soil pH reductions observed in Chinese soils. As such, it is capable of capturing the full
range of potential soil pH changes, from the low to high end, making it suitable for global
applications.
         Cumulative chemical nitrogen fertilizer inputs (over the period of 2020-2050) under three
future scenarios, in analogy to the above-mentioned SSPs, were projected by the GLOBIOM land use
model (65). Projections follow the scenarios established for the INMS project
(https://www.inms.international/) presented in ref (66), accounting for climate, development, land-
use, diet and N policy driven demand for nitrogen inputs. These three scenarios are SSP1-2.6-High-
Ambition-N policy scenario (i.e., sustainable development with high climate mitigation efforts and
with low level of mineral N inputs), SSP2-4.5-Medium-Ambition-N policy scenario (i.e., middle-of-
the-road socio-economic development with medium climate mitigation efforts and with medium level
of mineral N inputs), and SSP5-8.5-Low-Ambition-N policy scenario (i.e., fossil-fueled socio-
economic development without climate mitigation efforts and with high level of mineral N inputs).
Atmospheric nitrogen depositions under SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP5-8.5 were obtained from the CCMI
nitrogen deposition database initially prepared as the coordinated forcing datasets for CMIP6.
Synthesis of soil-mediated inorganic carbon fluxes. We quantified the present-day contribution of
soil-mediated inorganic carbon input into inland-waters (𝑓𝑆𝐼𝐶2𝑊 ) through two approaches (see Fig.
S19).
         The first approach utilized carbon inventories and derived 𝑓𝑆𝐼𝐶2𝑊 from subtracting total
carbon flux from land to inland-waters (𝑓𝐿2𝑊 ) by all known non-SIC sources. 𝑓𝐿2𝑊 ranged from 2.7 –
4.08 GtC yr -1 (5.1 in ref (67) was corrected to be 4.08 in ref (37) ) in literature(37, 67, 68), based on
carbon stock and output from inland-waters. As the most recent comprehensive synthesis, ref(37)
revised 𝑓𝐿2𝑊 to be 2.95 ± 0.28 (±1 sigma) GtC yr -1. This value, derived from multiple data sources
from both observations and models, represents the robust and most current estimate of 𝑓𝐿2𝑊 and is
used in our study. Known major sources that contribute to 𝑓𝐿2𝑊 include inorganic C export from
bedrock weathering (0.39 ± 0.06 GtC yr -1 )(37), dissolved organic carbon (DOC) export from
leaching and runoff (0.28 ± 0.04 GtC yr -1)(47), physical erosion of total recalcitrant particulate
inorganic carbon (PIC, 0.15 ± 0.02 GtC yr -1)(37), organic carbon export from fossil and old-soil (0.15
± 0.1 GtC yr -1)(37), organic C input from sewage to inland-waters (0.1 ± 0.02 GtC yr -1) (48) and
particulate organic carbon transferred through soil erosion (𝑓𝑒𝑟𝑜𝑠𝑖𝑜𝑛2𝑊 ). Ref (49) provides evidence
that earlier studies overestimated high values of 𝑓𝑒𝑟𝑜𝑠𝑖𝑜𝑛2𝑊 due to anthropogenic activities
(Supplementary material S1.1 of ref (49)). Specifically, ref (49) reports a value of 0.5 ± 0.08 GtC yr -1
attributed to erosions caused by water, wind, and tillage in crop and pasture lands. Anthropogenic
activities across agricultural land accounted for roughly two-thirds of the global erosion(49, 50). In
light of this evidence, we used an estimate of 0.75 ± 0. 11 GtC yr -1 for a reliable quantification of
𝑓𝑒𝑟𝑜𝑠𝑖𝑜𝑛2𝑊 . At last, the gap of 1.13 ± 0.33 (1 sigma) GtC yr -1 is attributable to 𝑓𝑆𝐼𝐶2𝑊 .
           The second approach estimated 𝑓𝑆𝐼𝐶2𝑊 by analyzing the gap in explaining riverine DIC
primarily through rock weathering, without adequately accounting for the contribution of SIC. Using
the expanded global database of riverine chemistry spanning all continents except Antarctica and
Greenland, the atmospheric CO2 consumption by rock weathering (𝑓𝐶𝑂2) is linked with riverine DIC
flux (𝑓𝐷𝐼𝐶2𝐸𝑂 , discharged from rivers to estuaries and oceans) as (see ref (69)),
                  𝑓𝐷𝐼𝐶2𝐸𝑂 = 0.996 + 2.214 × 𝑓𝐶𝑂2 (R2 =0.66) (unit: TgC yr-1)              (S4)
           At the global scale, 𝑓𝐷𝐼𝐶2𝐸𝑂 is around 410 TgC yr-1 (69). Therefore, Equation S4 can be
approximated as 𝑓𝐷𝐼𝐶2𝐸𝑂 = 2.214 × 𝑓𝐶𝑂2. From the carbonate chemistry, the uptake of one
atmospheric CO2 molecule during silicate weathering yields, at most, one molecule of HCO3- that
contributes to DIC in water, whereas the weathering of carbonate rocks results in at most two DIC
molecules per atmospheric CO2 molecule uptake. Silicate weathering accounts for 63% of the global
rock weathering flux (70). Therefore, every mole of atmospheric CO2 uptake generates at most 1.37
(𝑖. 𝑒. , 1 × 0.63 + 2 × 0.37) moles of DIC, that is
                                         𝑓𝐷𝐼𝐶2𝐸𝑂 = 1.37 × 𝑓𝐶𝑂2             (S5)
When considering only rock weathering, there is a gap of at least 0.844 × 𝑓𝐶𝑂2 (Equations S4 vs. S5).
We illustrate below that this 0.844 × 𝑓𝐶𝑂2 gap is unlikely to be gained in inland-waters.
           Present-day inland-waters are saturated in CO2 at the global scale, with CO2 outgassed from
inland-waters reaching 1.85 GtC yr -1 in ref (37), and 2.1 – 2. 5 in ref (48, 67) ( 3.7 in ref (67) was
corrected to be 2.5 in ref (37)). DOC and particulate organic carbon (POC) delivered from inland-
waters to the estuaries toward oceans are relatively well constrained at around 0.4 GtC yr -1 (69, 71).
Inland-water carbon burial is around 0.15 GtC yr -1 (37, 72). If DOC and POC are the only sources
that contribute to degassing, it requires at least 2.4 (i.e., 1.85 + 0.4 + 0.15) GtC yr -1 of DOC and POC
as input into inland-waters. Existing data shows that DOC and POC inputs into inland-waters are less
than 2.4 GtC yr -1 (DOC: 0.28; 𝑓𝑒𝑟𝑜𝑠𝑖𝑜𝑛2𝑊 : 0.75 GtC yr -1; see numbers from the first approach).
Altogether, there need inorganic carbon sources to close the carbon budget. The contribution of PIC to
𝑓𝐿2𝑊 is relatively small (0.15 GtC yr -1 in ref (37)) and in the same magnitude as PIC delivered toward
oceans (69). Therefore, at the global scale the 0.844 × 𝑓𝐶𝑂2 mismatch is attributable to unaccounted
input of DIC into inland-waters.
           Among the 2.8 GtC yr -1 (POC, DOC, DIC; PIC not included) exported from land to inland-
waters, 0.95 GtC yr -1 reached river mouths where the water enters estuaries and moves toward the
ocean (see ref (37)), resulting an input/delivery ratio of 2.95. As a coarse approximation, here we
assume the input/delivery ratio for DIC is the same as the overall value of 2.95. This value is
relatively conservative compared to 3.45 estimated from the total DIC input flux from the first
approach dividing the amount of DIC ((1.13 + 0.39)/0.44 ) exported toward the ocean. To keep the
second approach relatively independent from the first approach, we use the value of 2.95 for the
following estimations. A gap of 0.844 × 𝑓𝐶𝑂2 in 𝑓𝐷𝐼𝐶2𝐸𝑂 at delivery corresponds to a gap of 2.49 ×
𝑓𝐶𝑂2 at input from land to inland-waters that is unaccounted, i.e., at least 0.72 GtC yr -1 (𝑓𝐶𝑂2 is
around 0.29 GtC yr -1, see ref (37)) of DIC exported to inland-waters that could not be explained by
the estimation through chemical weathering, which is based mostly on rock distributions. Note that
this value represents a conservative lower bound on the actual 𝑓𝑆𝐼𝐶2𝑊 , as processes such as retention
of the weathering products in soils, consumption of DIC (e.g., uptake by vegetation and CO2 evasion)
sourced from weathering in inland-waters, would contribute to an underestimate of the real value.
           To account for processes affecting rock-weathering-sourced DIC in inland-waters, we applied
an input/delivery ratio of 2.95 to the rock weathering flux (now, 𝑓𝐷𝐼𝐶2𝐸𝑂 = 1.37 × 𝑓𝐶𝑂2 /2.95 ),
which gave us a mismatch of (2.214 − 1.37/2.95) 𝑓𝐶𝑂2 in calculating 𝑓𝐷𝐼𝐶2𝐸𝑂 . Using the similar
calculation as described earlier, 𝑓𝑆𝐼𝐶2𝑊 reached 1.5 GtC yr -1 that could not be explained by
weathering flux derived primarily from rocks.
           There are several sources of uncertainties in the second approach that we did not quantify. A
clear separation of soil from rock is difficult. Global chemical weathering estimates typically rely on
modelled weathering fluxes that are upscaled by distributions of lithological classes. SIC was not
explicitly accounted for in quantifying the global chemical weathering, while in some models,
parameterization through riverine chemistry to some extent indirectly (might also incorrectly) reflects
contributions from soil minerals. The chemical weathering flux applied in this study brought limited
signal from SIC as the weathering model was parameterized in locations with low SIC content(69,
70). On the other hand, if Ca2+ released from rock weathering stays in soil, 𝑓𝑆𝐼𝐶2𝑊 should be larger
than the estimation (1.5 GtC yr -1) assuming all weathering fluxes reach inland-waters. There are also
uncertainties associated with the empirical relationship presented in Equation S4. To be conservative,
we report here that at least 1.13 ± 0.33 (1 sigma) GtC is transferred to inland-waters every year via
soils, with the second approach serving as an independent line of evidence to support the robustness
of the first approach. Note here, by inland-waters, we partially accounted for the groundwater that are
headwater of streams, but might not all groundwater that might be affected by SIC.
         From carbonate chemistry, for every mole of CO2 taken up, 2 moles of HCO3- (DIC) are
produced. To deliver 1.13 GtC yr -1 of DIC (if all sourced from carbonates, which is not true in
reality) to inland-waters, 0.56 GtC yr -1 of CO2 is taken up through SIC dissolution. However, SIC
dissolution can also occur under highly acidic conditions, generating CO2 instead of HCO3-. It has
been reported that 12-38% of the total dissolved SIC contributes to CO2 degassing (30, 31, 34, 35).
Taking the median value of 25% as the estimate of the degassing fraction, we estimated a degassing
flux of 0.19 GtC yr-1. Consequently, carbonate dissolution resulted in a net uptake of atmospheric
carbon of 0.37 GtC yr-1 by soil through the inorganic pathway. Of the 1.13 GtC yr-1 that enter inland-
waters, 0.71 GtC yr-1 are released from inland-waters to the atmosphere, assuming an input/delivery
ratio of 2.95 as previously mentioned. We estimated that 0.057 GtC yr -1 from inland-water burial
came from SIC-sourced carbon, out of a total of 0.15 GtC yr -1, by the portion of input from SIC vs.
the total input into inland-waters. The contribution of SIC-sourced carbon to oceanic carbon is
therefore 0.36 GtC annually. The loss of SIC acted as a source of 0.34 GtC yr-1 to the atmosphere
when considering both soil and inland-waters as “land” if we assume that all 𝑓𝑆𝐼𝐶2𝑊 come from
carbonates. In this study, we use the term “terrestrial ecosystem” to differentiate it from “land”, which
includes inland-waters in our context. The unaccounted land-atmospheric CO2 exchange from SIC is
likely implicitly incorporated as part of terrestrial ecosystem-biosphere carbon fluxes sourced from
the soil. In Fig. 4C, we separated the original terrestrial ecosystem carbon into terrestrial ecosystem
(organic) and soil (inorganic) (Fig. 4C).
         SIC-atmosphere and SIC-inland-water fluxes estimated in this study did not explicitly
distinguish between naturally formed SIC and anthropogenic additions through liming that could
contribute to these fluxes. Additionally, we did not quantify the indirect effect of SIC such as on soil
organic carbon stabilization (11), primary productivity (8, 73, 74), and the burial of organic matter in
aquatic ecosystems (“biological pump”) (75). While aquatic organisms rely on DIC or mineralization
of DOC to support photosynthesis and organic matter production, the relative contribution of these
sources is not known. Nonetheless, organic carbon buried in inland-waters is around 0.15 GtC yr-1
(37), and the indirect carbon sequestration resulting from source of SIC is not significant enough to
change the overall contribution of soil-mediated inorganic carbon as a source of CO2. For weathering
carbon fluxes, we did not separate the amount that goes directly (surface rocks) into inland-waters
versus the amount that contributes to SIC first and then enters the inland-waters. As a first step, we
reconstructed a plausible SIC-related major carbon fluxes, as presented in Fig. 4C, based on estimates
in limited literature, and a more nuanced approach is necessary to fully assess the robustness and
uncertainties associated with these fluxes.

     1. Standardization and integration of measured SIC
      Soil inorganic carbon (SIC) records from the ISRIC World Soil Information Service (WoSIS)
included 268,783 samples from 51,925 sites. We standardized this database through removing
irrelevant layers, consistency check of sampling depth, removing soil profiles without information of
calcium carbonate equivalent and removing samples measured before 1970. We deleted the profile
with NA data and used the averages of SIC values of the same layers from all profiles. At last, we
obtained 198,944 samples from 47,364 sites collected by WoSIS.
       Consistency of sampling depth for one soil profile means that “upper_depth” (upper depth of
layer) of the first layer is zero, both “upper_depth” and “lower_depth” (lower depth of layer) are not
NULL and should be greater than zero, and “lower_depth” should be greater than “upper_depth”. We
removed “litter layers” in the WoSIS database before checking the consistency of sampling depths, as
they are not soils per se. A small portion of sampling depths were not consecutive and were revised
accordingly.
       Depth values are negative. We manually revised them as corresponding positive values.
       There are two or more layers starting from 0 for one soil profile. We deleted one layer.
       The first layer does not start from 0. We set the “upper_depth” of the first layer to 0.
           “lower_depth” is zero. These values were manually revised.
       The information on the “upper_depth” and “lower_depth” is missing (NA). If both
           “upper_depth” and “lower_depth” are missing, we delete these layers. If one profile only has
           one topmost layer, we manually set the “lower_depth” as 20 cm. Otherwise, the
           “lower_depth” is set by adding 30 cm and 50 cm for “upper_depth” less and greater than
           100 cm, respectively, considering the increments of sampling depth of subsoils are generally
           greater than topsoils.
       “upper_depth” is greater than “lower_depth”. We manually flipped these depths, or removed
           this soil layer if this layer is redundant and removing it does not affect the consecutiveness
           of this profile. Otherwise, the depth values were revised according to the depth increments
           of adjacent upper and below layers.
       The values of the “upper_depth” and “lower_depth” are the same in adjacent layers. We
           deleted these redundant layers. However, we found some layers need to be checked
           manually according to the vertical variation of carbonate contents and manually revised
           these profiles.
       We included covariates such as soil pH (water), sand, silt, clay, soil organic carbon content, bulk
density, volumetric content of coarse fragments (>2 mm), cation exchange capacity (buffered at pH7),
total soil nitrogen, total soil phosphorus content, Bray1 phosphorus and electrical conductivity from
WoSIS. For samples reporting gravimetric content of coarse fragments, we converted it to volumetric
content of coarse fragments according to Ribeiro et al. (2020). For bulk density (BD), we remapped
BD of the fine earth fraction equilibrated at 33 kPa (BDFI33) to BD of the fine earth fraction
measured by oven dry (BDFIOD) using the pedotransfer function (PTF) between BDFI33 and
BDFIOD. We built the PTF through samples measuring both BDFI33 and BDFIOD (N=40,470). We
fitted six frequently applied linear and non-linear functions and used the PTF that yielded the highest
R-squared (Fig. S21). The R2 values ranged from 0.55 to 0.67. A linear function is adopted, i.e.,
BDFIOD=0.1736+0.9197BDFI33 (R2: 0.67) (Fig. S21)
       We supplemented WoSIS database with local studies compiled from literature, country-level
inventories and coordinated field campaigns. We standardized these datasets following the same
procedure as for WoSIS and added 24,649 samples from 7,713 sites across the globe (Fig. S2). Ref (4,
52) provided additional information about datasets from China, Ref (53) from Germany, Ref (54)
from Egypt. Additional dataset from Russia were measured and provided by co-authors. We also
searched the literature and added data sources that were not in our constructed database. Fig. S1
shows the searching algorithm and procedures. References to this literature extracted data are
provided in Table S12.
     2. Comparison with other large-scale estimations of SIC storage.
       Existing estimation of the global total SIC budget (Table S1) varies among studies partly due to
the differences in soil depth and land area covered by different studies. Earlier estimations were based
on upscaling the mean SIC content by the area of land/soil units. From the pioneer work of
Schlesinger 1982 (2) to more recent study of Batjes 2016 (76), the number of SIC measurements has
been increasing (from around 90 to 21,000 soil profiles) and the map of land/soil unit is more detailed,
moving from very coarse to relatively high resolution (e.g., ~30 seconds). Traditional land/soil unit-
based mapping methods predicted soil properties according to soil units (i.e., soil types), with the
underlying assumption that soil is homogeneous within soil units that belong to the same class/type
(Tables S1, S12). Notably, some information might be lost on these polygon-based maps, as the
production of traditional soil maps required soil information to be condensed and data to be classified.
Our estimation and mapping of SIC is not based on soil units. Soils belonging to the same soil unit but
from different places might have different SIC from our estimation considering the heterogeneity
nature of soil. Our estimation links SIC to the particular local environment represented by around 60
environmental covariates. SIC storage estimated from our study is less homogeneous compared to
earlier studies. In addition, with the compiled databases with a much larger number of measurements
(223,593 measurements from 55,077 soil profiles), our estimation is expected to reflect the real world
more closely.
      The mean SIC of Aridisols from our study reaches 39 kg m-2, which is between 66 (calcareous)
and 33 (non-calcareous) kg m-2 from desert soils used in earlier global estimations that upscaled
averages from limited number of soil profiles by areas of interpretive soil types/units (1, 2, 20) (Table
S1). For country-level budgets, our estimate is comparable with values compiled from literature
(Table S5). A wider distribution of SIC (e.g., in humid region, or local variations that are
misrepresented by soil mapping types/units), or the higher content of SIC in deeper soils make our
global budget larger than earlier global estimations (Tables S1, S4, S6). The high accuracy of our
data-derived models in predicting out-of-sample values ensures that our estimation closely reflects
patterns embedded in observations (Figs. 1, 2).
      Our map shows the existence of SIC around central Africa because in our complied database, the
content of SIC is relatively large in some part of the central Africa (Fig. 1). These data points come
from the World Soil Information Service (WoSIS) Soil Profile Database (Fig. S2), which has been
carefully harmonized and standardized by the international community. In regions where we predicted
non-trivial SIC, i.e., around the border of Cameroon, Central African Republic, Republic of the
Congo, and Democratic Republic of the Congo, the carbonate rock, karst environment (77) , alluvial
deposits (Fig. S5) and dust deposition (78) likely contribute to the presence of SIC. Examining the
Köppen–Geiger climate classification reveals a tropical savanna and monsoon climate in this area,
distinguishing it from a tropical rainforest climate (79). That being said, we acknowledge that there
are uncertainties in assessing the extent and magnitude of SIC content around central Africa when
observations are limited. We recognize that additional observations and measurements will contribute
to minimizing these uncertainties.
     Soils with no SIC in the top soil (0-30cm, SIC < 0.1 kg m-2), but accumulations of SIC in deeper
soils (30-100cm, or 100-200cm, SIC > 0.1 kg m-2) were predicted to spread across regions (Fig. S10).
A considerable portion of Russian soils is characterized by lack of SIC in the top soil, and
accumulation of SIC in deeper soils. The prediction is supported by local studies, for example, in
Western Siberia(80), South Siberia (81), Central Yakutia (82) or of forest soils in Russia (83), and
also by the humus-carbonate soils that have been documented as part of Russian soils (84). For these
soils, humus accumulates on top of soils developing from cation-rich material such as carbonate
parent material or loess dust.
     3. Predictors of SIC other than soil pH
      The storage of SIC is shaped by multiple factors interacting with each other. Acidity, especially
in concurrent with sufficient water, accelerates losses of SIC. The importance of soil pH emerged
from data due to the strong coupling of pH and the carbonate system (Equations S1, S2). The presence
of SIC is also affected by variations in temperature. Soils located in regions with a low temperature
seasonality and annual temperature range smaller than 25 °C (e.g., the tropics) are associated with
highly weathered and well-developed soils that are more likely to be depleted of SIC. Cation
exchange capacity (CEC), a measure of the capacity of soil to supply cations (e.g., calcium,
magnesium and potassium) is ranked among the top six factors explaining the global pattern of the
presence of SIC, in line with the importance of cation in the formation of solid carbonate. In addition
to factors listed above, numerous edaphic, climatic, topographic and anthropogenic factors contribute
to develop the global pattern of the distributions of SIC.
      Soil pH stands out as the top important predictor explaining the size of SIC from SIC-bearing
soils. In addition, cation exchange capacity, Palmer drought severity index, soil texture, phosphorus
deposition (likely correlates with aeolian dust carries both phosphorus and calcium) and volumetric
coarse fragments are also among the top 6 most important predictors of the magnitude of SIC across
the globe (Fig. 3). Their relationship with SIC show different degrees of non-linearities (Fig. 3). These
six predictors together explain 28.9% of the total captured variations, leaving the remaining variations
explained by factors such as soil clay content, soil depth, precipitation of the warmest quarter, soil
sand content, wind speed, nitrogen deposition rate (nhx), soil organic matter content, temperature
seasonality, precipitation seasonality, precipitation of wettest quarter, temperature annual range,
reference evapotranspiration (FAO 56 Penman-Monteith Method), and the bulk density.

    4. Derive pH-SIC curve.
     To isolate the relationship between pH and SIC content, we calculated the contribution of pH in
explaining variations of SIC through estimation the gain of SIC (deviation from the mean value)
explained by pH for each soil sample, in short, 𝑆𝐼𝐶𝑝𝐻 . We quantified 𝑆𝐼𝐶𝑝𝐻 as the marginal
contribution (estimation with a particular pH value vs. the average from all combinations of predictor
space without that pH value) indicated by Shapley values. The density plot of pH vs. 𝑆𝐼𝐶𝑝𝐻 displays a
S-shape (Fig. 3). We fitted samples by a 5-parameter sigmoid function. To avoid numerical issues of
curve-fitting, we separated pH (from 4 to 10) into 6000 equally distributed bins and calculated the
means that we used for curve fitting (Figs. S11, S12; Table S9).

    6. Pattern of the pH-SIC curve.
      Soils from multiple soil orders, under different vegetation covers and in different soil depths
generally follow a similar sigmoid-type curve for the relationship between soil pH and 𝑆𝐼𝐶𝑝𝐻 . Under
acidic conditions, the curve is relatively flat, followed by steep changes in the pH range between 7
and 8.5, and then plateaus and remains relatively stable. The sigmoid-type curve reflects theoretical
understandings of the carbonate chemistry underlying calcium carbonate preservation. Acidic
conditions favor calcium carbonate dissolution that limits the accumulation of SIC. The magnitude of
increment of solubility of calcium carbonate with pH is very small under strong alkaline (e.g., > 8.5)
conditions, making further increasing pH have a small impact on SIC. On the other hand, SIC content
beyond the saturation point in soil solution no longer contributes to variations in soil pH, making pH
value larger than the equilibrium pH (with calcite dissolution) less indicative for solid carbonate
content. The overall (global) pH-SIC curve emerges as the integrated outcome of carbonate chemistry
and the world’s soil environment. The principles of carbonate chemistry play the first order role in
SIC distribution and accumulation, which are compounded by the sources of Ca2+, climatic,
topographic, biological, and edaphic conditions. We found deviations from the global pH-SIC curve
when samples were grouped by soil orders, soil depths and land cover types.
      Among different soil orders, Oxisols, characterized by low cation exchange capacity and low
weatherable minerals to buffer pH, shows the most abrupt (large k value) changes of SIC in response
to soil pH, while Alfisols and Mollisols display the most smooth (small k value, Equation S6; Table
S9) changes in the pH-SIC curve, given their capability to neutralize H+ from sources other than SIC
(e.g., organic matter and clay minerals). Generally speaking, most Mollisols have had grass vegetation
at some stage. Melanization (the process of darkening of the soil by organic matter additions) is one
of the most important processes in the formation of a Mollisol. Mollisols are generally fertile and rich
in organic matter, which can function to buffer H+, for example, through the formation of Ca-humic
materials, or base-rich clays. Alfisols often contain significant amounts of clay minerals, such as
kaolinite, illite, and montmorillonite. Clay minerals have a high cation exchange capacity, which
means they can hold onto and exchange positively charged ions (cations), including hydrogen ions
(H+). This property helps buffer changes in soil pH. The sensitivity of SIC to pH displays a bell shape,
with the maximum sensitivity at pH = 7.89, and this value varies from 7.86 to 8.04 across different
soil orders. Despite a large portion of the global SIC accumulated in Aridisols, Aridisols hold less SIC
under strong alkaline conditions than other soil orders.
       Along soil depths, topsoil (0-0.3m) shows more abrupt (larger k value) transitions in the pH-SIC
curve than the middle (0.3-1m) and bottom (1 – 2 m) soil layers. pH results in a smaller range of
variations in SIC (b/a in Equation S6) in topsoil than deeper soil layers. Under strong alkaline
conditions, deeper soils hold more SIC than the topsoil.
       Between tree and grass, soils under the tree show a smoother transition in the pH-SIC curve. pH
results in a larger range of variations in SIC (b/a) with tree cover than under grass Under strong
alkaline conditions.
     7. Convergence of the pH-SIC curve.
       Compared to the global pH-SIC curve, the differences within soil orders, soil depths or
vegetation types are smallest around soil pH = 8.0 (Fig. S13), a value that is close to the theoretical
pH value of 8.2 when the soil solution equilibrium with calcite dissolution (Supplementary Text 5).
The divergences increase as pH moves away from the minimal point at pH = 8.0. Under acidic
conditions, the variation among different soil conditions is large and remains relatively constant as
SIC does not vary much with soil pH. The deviations peak shortly after pH =7 with initially differed
sensitivity of SIC to soil pH.
